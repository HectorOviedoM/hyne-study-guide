Chapter 10 — Reservoir Rocks

Not every sedimentary rock is a reservoir. This chapter is about which sands and carbonates actually store and flow—and how depositional setting, diagenesis, and fractures decide whether a layer is pay or tight.

Learning goals

  • Link sorting and depositional setting to sandstone reservoir quality.
  • Contrast limestone vs dolomite porosity styles (reef, oolite, karst, chalk).
  • Explain when fractures turn “useless” rock into a reservoir.
  • Map reservoir/seal language to how tops and facies show up in well data.

Core definitions

  • Reservoir rock — porous and permeable rock that holds producible oil or gas.
  • Shale — fine-grained, low-permeability mudrock; can be source, seal, or the rock that encases the reservoir.
  • Source rock — usually organic shale where HC were generated.
  • Caprock — impermeable layer above the reservoir that stops upward escape.
  • Reef / channel — carbonate buildup or sand-filled river path that can be reservoir encased in shale.
  • Trap — reservoir + seal + geometry that stops migration.
  • Primary stratigraphic trap — reservoir deposited already sealed by shale; no later fold/fault required.

Sandstone reservoir rocks

Sorting is the grain-size range. Well-sorted rocks have similar grain sizes (often called clean sands) and better quality; poorly sorted mixes sizes and usually hurts porosity and permeability.

Dune sandstones

Wind in deserts and coasts suspends only a narrow grain size → naturally well-sorted piles. Buried dunes can become excellent reservoirs (classic eolian examples in the Rockies and North Sea / Groningen gas systems).

Shoreline sandstones

Beach sands: waves winnow silt and clay seaward, leaving a long, narrow belt of clean sand. Buried beach trends can be reservoirs sealed by flanking shale (stratigraphic traps).

River (meandering) sandstones

In meanders, fast outer banks erode; slow inner banks drop coarser sand as point bars—well-sorted, porous pay. Abandoned channels fill with clay (clay plugs → shale seals). Oil migrates updip in the sand until it hits that shale. Sometimes an entire channel fairway produces as a long, narrow reservoir.

Delta sandstones

River meets sea or lake and builds a fan of sediment with distributary channels. Constructive deltas (river-dominated) push lobes seaward; destructive deltas (wave-dominated) leave flatter, beachier fronts. Why oil shows up: rivers fertilize seas → algae → black shale ahead (source); beach and channel sands above (reservoir); compaction drives migration into those sands.

Granite wash

Not live granite—coarse sand left when granite weathers at surface. Because granite already breaks into sand-sized, fairly uniform grains, wash can be well-sorted and coarse. Oil/gas generate deeper in a source and migrate up into the wash; structure (e.g., anticline) can focus accumulation.

Carbonate reservoir rocks

Carbonates are limestone (CaCO₃) and dolomite—not siliciclastic sand. Limestone is secreted by marine organisms or precipitated from water, mostly in warm shallow seas. “Limestone” alone is not a reservoir: you need porosity (reef fabric, ooids, karst, dolomite). Lime mud (micrite) is generally not reservoir.

Reefs and platforms

A reef is a wave-resistant mound of shells and carbonate framework (corals today; sponges, algae, rudists in the past). It grows in clear, warm, shallow water. Primary fabric pores enlarge when freshwater dissolves limestone. Shale or salt above → primary stratigraphic trap. The inner lagoon deposits micrite—usually non-pay.

Shapes:

  • Barrier — long wall parallel to shore with a lagoon behind.
  • Atoll — ring with central lagoon (oil often in the outer rim; center micrite).
  • Pinnacle — small steep cone; often salt-sealed; small footprint → hard to hit without good imaging.

A limestone platform is a vast shallow carbonate shelf. Strong tides can precipitate CaCO₃ spheres (oolites) → oolitic limestone with good primary porosity. Platform rims of broken shells or ooids can be beach-like reservoirs.

Karst, chalk, dolomite

Karst limestone: rainwater + CO₂ makes carbonic acid that dissolves limestone into vugs (cm-scale holes) or caves—major porosity in many Middle East systems. Karst often sits just below an angular unconformity (old erosion surface). Caves are hard to see on seismic; wells may hit them by chance.

Chalk: ultra-fine limestone from microfossils. High porosity, very low permeability (tiny pores). Produces only when fractured.

Dolomite: does not deposit like limestone—it replaces limestone when Mg-rich water swaps Ca for Mg. Harder and less soluble, so it loses porosity less on burial; larger crystals can widen pore throats → higher permeability. If the limestone was impermeable micrite, dolomite may appear only along fractures. Mixed karst + dolomite can create cavernous pay.

Fractured reservoirs

Any rock that is broken can produce even if matrix quality is poor. Anticline crests crack brittle basement; oil may rise from a deeper source into fractured basement and adjacent sands. Chert/shale packages, fractured chalk, and even weathered/fractured volcanic rocks (drillers sometimes call certain altered volcanics “serpentine”) can be reservoirs when joints provide the permeability network.

Data engineer / WITSML

  • Reservoir tops and bases are usually picked on logs (GR/Res/porosity) and stored as markers—compare in TVD across wells.
  • Facies (channel vs shale, reef vs lagoon) are interpretations; raw curves do not label “point bar” for you.
  • Core porosity/permeability calibrate log φ; fractures may show on image logs or sonic amplitude, not on simple GR.
  • well → many wellbores may penetrate different parts of a reef or channel fairway.

Key terms

Clean sand
Well-sorted sandstone with little clay.
Point bar
Inner-bend fluvial sand deposit—often good pay.
Reef
Wave-resistant carbonate buildup; fabric porosity.
Karst / vug
Dissolution porosity; cm holes to caves.
Chalk
Fine microfossil limestone; needs fractures to flow.
Dolomite
Mg-replaced limestone; often better throats/perm.
Granite wash
Weathered granite sand—potential reservoir.
