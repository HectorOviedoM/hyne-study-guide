Chapter 14 — Drilling Preliminaries

Before the bit turns, someone picks a target, secures rights, budgets the well, and signs contracts. This chapter is the business and planning layer that turns a geologic idea into a spud date—and into the well identity you will later see in data systems.

Learning goals

  • Explain fee land mineral vs surface rights, leases, royalty, and working interest.
  • Contrast concession, PSC, and service-style foreign contracts.
  • Read what an AFE covers and how risk-adjusted ROI / payout / IRR screen prospects.
  • Distinguish footage, daywork, and turnkey drilling contracts; list well-type labels (wildcat → infill).

Land and leasing

In the USA and parts of Canada, private fee land often splits surface rights (farm, house) from mineral rights (explore, drill, own oil/gas). They need not be the same owner. Elsewhere, minerals usually belong to the state. Roughly one-third of US land is public (federal/state); in Canada, state land is crown land.

A landman finds mineral owners (county records, title companies, lease maps, attorney title opinions) and gets a lease: right to explore and drill while the lease lives. The owner (lessor) receives a bonus at signing and a royalty (fraction of gross oil/gas, no production costs—historically 1/8, often higher today). The company (lessee) holds a primary term (often 1–5 years). Without commercial production “in paying quantities” by the end, the lease expires; with it, a secondary term lasts for the life of production.

Lease forms may include delay rental (pay each year of primary term if not spudded, or lose the lease) or paid-up (no delay rental). Leases are property—bought, sold, traded. Farm out: pass the lease to another company that will drill, for royalty or other consideration; what you receive is a farm in.

On a producing well: royalty interest (gross fraction, no costs—landowner royalty, sometimes overriding royalties carved from working interest) vs working interest (pays to drill and operate; keeps what remains after royalties and opex). After all royalties, net revenue interest remains (e.g., 87.5%). At completion the operator issues a division order (who gets paid what %).

Foreign contracts

Multinational / international companies operate in many countries; national / host companies usually stay in one. Phases: exploration (geology, geochem, seismic, wildcats), exploitation (develop the new field), production. Contracts cover a concession area for a contract term.

  • Concession / license / tax and royalty: company takes cost and risk; country takes bonus, taxes, royalties. Variant: company explores alone; host shares exploitation cost/risk.
  • Production-sharing contract (common today): company explores/drills at its cost. No commercial by contract end → contract dies, costs lost. If commercial: cost oil recovers exploration/drilling/production costs; remaining profit oil splits by formula.
  • Service contract: fee for a service (explore or produce).
  • Production contract: improve an existing or weak field; keep a % of the incremental oil.

Authority for Expenditure and economics

An AFE estimates well cost as dry hole and as producer: drilling intangibles, completion intangibles, and equipment. Intangibles = wages, services, and items with little salvage. Includes rig, mud, logs, tests, cement, casing, stimulation, pumps, tubing, separator, etc. Operator and funding parties sign it; it guides spending. Overruns happen—famous wells have blown far past initial AFE.

Two numbers dominate screening: risk and reserves. Chance of success (e.g., 0.65) comes from area history and well type, or from geo assessment of reservoir + seal + trap. Reserves estimate how much that well can produce.

  • ROI: net revenue / peak capital for drill & complete, discounted because revenue arrives later. ROI × chance of success = risk-adjusted ROI.
  • Payout (PO): time until net revenue equals spend (also discounted).
  • IRR: discount rate that equates life-of-well net revenue to drill/complete cost. Many firms set a minimum IRR hurdle.

Best prospects: high risk-adjusted ROI and short discounted payout.

Drilling contracts

A drilling contractor owns the rig. The contract sets depth or horizon (drilling target), location, spud date, hole sizes, max deviation from vertical, mud, logs/tests, casing sizes and depths, cement, completion style, collars, formations—and standby/logging rates when not drilling.

  • Footage: $/ft to contract depth (typical onshore).
  • Daywork: $/day (typical offshore).
  • Turnkey: fixed price to depth; sometimes includes complete/equip per tests.
  • Combination: footage to X, daywork below.

Forms often follow API/AAODC styles. Service company = one service (wireline, mud); supply company = hardware (casing).

JOA and support agreements

A JOA (joint operating agreement) splits a well or area’s working interest: rights, cost %, who is operator (day-to-day), and how production is shared.

Support / contribution agreements:

  • Dry-hole: pay if dry; get geologic/drilling data either way.
  • Bottom-hole contribution: pay if a depth is reached; get data.
  • Acreage contribution: contribute leases/interests for data.

Site preparation

A surveyor stakes exact location and elevation; plat is filed. Dozer builds road, turnaround, and a ~4–6 acre pad (often gravel; wet sites use matting). Lined reserve pit holds excess mud, cuttings, waste. Water from a water well or pipeline.

Deeper wells need rigs that can rack more pipe. Each rig has a maximum depth rating. Shallow: truck-mounted. Deeper: trailer modules. Very deep: cellar under the floor for BOPs. Remote: helirig. Assemble = rig up; tear down = rig down.

Spudding in starts the well. Medium/deep wells often use a small truck rig for a conductor hole (tens of feet) and cement large conductor casing—supports the wellhead and anchors BOPs if shallow gas appears. Soft ground may allow pile-driving conductor without drilling.

Types of wells

  • Wildcat / exploratory — seeks a new reservoir.
    • New-field exploratory — no production in the area.
    • New-pool exploratory — another unproduced layer in a producing area (shallower or deeper pool test).
    • Outpost / extension / step-out — significantly extends field or reservoir limits.
  • Rank wildcat — ≥ ~2 miles (3 km) from known production.
  • Discovery well — finds a field.

After discovery, delineation / appraisal / step-out wells size the field (and decide which leases to keep). Hitting OWC or GWC on multiple sides outlines area. Developmental wells sit inside a known field; infill wells between producers raise rate.

Government regulations (spacing)

Modern rules assign a DSU (drilling and spacing unit)—typically one completed well per square/rectangle. Oil DSUs often 10, 20, or 40 acres (smaller if viscous oil or low perm). Gas drains farther—640-acre units are common. Wells need not be centered but cannot sit on the boundary. An allowable may cap oil/gas a well or field can produce in a period.

Cable-tool note

Early commercial US wells used cable tool: a chisel bit on a rope pounded by a walking beam; bail cuttings every few feet. Very slow, poor pressure control, blowouts common. Rotary largely replaced it for speed. For modern WITSML/time-log workflows (circulating mud, ROP, SPP), cable tool is not the model.

Data engineer / WITSML

  • Spud creates the well; each hole path is a wellbore (original, sidetrack, relief).
  • Well type (wildcat, development, appraisal) is metadata—useful for risk and analytics, not a log curve.
  • AFE and JOA live in financial/land systems; link by well identifier.
  • Location/elevation from survey become well header fields (KB, GL, coordinates).

Key terms

Lease / bonus / royalty
Right to drill; signing money; cost-free production share.
Working interest
Cost-bearing share of a well/field.
AFE
Authority for Expenditure—approved well cost estimate.
JOA
Joint operating agreement among working-interest owners.
Wildcat
Exploratory well for a new reservoir.
DSU
Drilling and spacing unit—legal well density.
Spud
Start of drilling the well.
